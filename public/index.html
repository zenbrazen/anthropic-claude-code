MedGraph: Maintaining Patient State with Graph Memory for Long-Term Medical Dialogue

Keeping medical AI from forgetting or mixing up patient histories

Donghua Zhang, Zizheng Wang, Ze Dong et al.
arXiv:2611.08433

Summary

Medical AI assistants often botch patient histories by retrieving outdated information, confusing one patient's facts with another's, or missing critical safety details. Researchers built MedGraph, a memory system that stores patient facts with dates, ties them to the right person, and links related health records into chains—letting AI answer questions about what was true at a specific point in time. On a benchmark of longitudinal medical conversations, MedGraph outperformed five other memory systems by 10+ points.

Why it matters

Medical AI that misremembers patient histories can lead to dangerous errors—prescribing medications to the wrong person or acting on outdated diagnoses. Better memory means AI assistants can reliably track patients across multiple visits without requiring doctors to re-enter information or second-guess the AI's recollection. This directly reduces the friction and risk that currently limits AI use in routine clinical care.

Zirah: A Post-Quantum True Random Number Generator Hardware Signer With an Avalanche Noise

Building a cheap hardware signer that reveals where quantum-resistant keys leak

Muhammad Shaheer Bin Junaid
arXiv:2611.08200

Summary

Researchers built a $50-class device that signs documents using quantum-resistant cryptography while deliberately exposing its vulnerabilities. They found that the post-quantum signing key, stored in the device's ordinary memory, could be extracted by a debugger before protections were fully enabled—demonstrating that even when the random number generator works perfectly, the key itself remains exposed on inexpensive hardware.

Why it matters

As quantum computers threaten current encryption, organizations are switching to post-quantum algorithms. This work shows that the algorithm's strength doesn't matter if the hardware storing the signing key is cheap and has debug ports left accessible. It reveals a concrete attack path that manufacturers and users need to defend against, not as a theoretical concern but as something an attacker with physical access can actually execute.

Invariance before structure: two modes of emergent compression in representation learning

Why different brains learn the same patterns, in two separate stages

Hardik Rajpal, Dan Goodman
arXiv:2611.07952

Summary

Neural networks—both biological and artificial—develop strikingly similar internal representations despite having completely different hardware and random starting points. Researchers identified two distinct phases: first, networks compress information in ways independent of any task (parameter-space compression), then 30 epochs later, they reorganize to capture the actual structure of what they're learning (state-space compression). This explains why brains and machines converge on the same solutions even when built from entirely different parts.

Why it matters

Understanding how invariance emerges helps explain why evolution and deep learning produce similar neural solutions—and why that similarity appears on a predictable timeline. This could improve how we design artificial neural networks by revealing which structures form automatically versus which require task-specific learning, making it easier to predict and control what representations will emerge in new systems.

Label-Wording Augmentation in a Typed-Decision Encoder: Gains on Unfamiliar Words, Not on Abbreviations or Codes

How rewording routing options helps AI understand real workplace language

Vladimir Beskorovainyi
arXiv:2611.08439

Summary

When AI systems must choose from options that users supply in varying ways, rewording those options during training helps the model handle unfamiliar words and slang—but not abbreviations or coded labels. Testing two versions of a Russian language model on routing tasks showed the reworded version improved accuracy by up to 24 percentage points on everyday language, though gains disappeared entirely when labels were opaque codes or abbreviations.

Why it matters

Chatbots and AI assistants often route requests to the right department or service based on labels they see at request time—labels the AI builders didn't create and can't control. This work shows that training on multiple phrasings of those labels makes the system more robust to how humans actually word things in offices and customer service, though it won't help when labels are meaningless codes. That means AI systems deployed in real workplaces can become more reliable without needing to standardize how people label their options.

Beyond Anonymous Order Flow: Wallet Identity as a Feature for Short-Horizon Return Prediction

Using trader identities to predict cryptocurrency price movements seconds ahead

Jingqiu Ding, Daojing Zhai
arXiv:2611.05218

Summary

Researchers analyzed 17.1 billion trades on Hyperliquid and found that tracking individual wallet addresses — rather than just treating all trades anonymously — improves short-term price predictions. In their best model, identifying the top 10% of traders by past performance and monitoring their activity raised prediction accuracy by 1.12 percentage points and generated profits of 3.11 basis points per trade over ten seconds.

Why it matters

This shows that in crypto markets, individual trader identity matters for predicting price moves in the next few seconds to minutes. Since cryptocurrency exchanges now expose wallet addresses while traditional stock markets keep trader identity hidden, crypto traders have access to information that stock traders don't — creating a meaningful edge for those who know how to use it. This could widen the gap between sophisticated algorithmic traders and ordinary investors in crypto markets.

Same Behavior, Different Mechanism: What Finite Experiments Can Certify About Integrated Information

Can we measure how a machine thinks by watching what it does?

Linqian Wu, Diaoxuan He
arXiv:2611.08469

Summary

Two AI systems can behave identically to an outside observer yet process information completely differently inside. Researchers show that detecting these hidden differences requires careful internal recordings—and that some internal structures simply cannot be reliably measured from outside, no matter how much data you collect. The study reveals fundamental limits on what we can certify about how AI systems actually work.

Why it matters

As AI systems take on higher-stakes roles, auditors and regulators need ways to verify that a system's internal reasoning is sound, not just that it produces correct outputs. This work establishes which internal properties are measurable at all and which are structurally invisible from external observation—directly shaping what claims about AI safety and consciousness we can actually prove.

Dynamical effects induced by synthetic SU(1,1) gauge fields

How artificial force fields amplify and entangle quantum particles

Chenwei Lv
arXiv:2611.08447

Summary

Physicists discovered that specially designed gauge fields can make quantum particles oscillate in unstable ways that amplify their behavior, rather than keeping them stable. The amplification follows geometric rules in curved spacetime, and threading this field through a system of atoms creates a model where entanglement—the quantum connection between particles—scales in unexpected ways that depends on whether the field is stable or unstable.

Why it matters

The findings offer a new way to control how quantum information spreads and entangles in systems that lose energy to their surroundings. Because the framework applies to different types of force fields and higher dimensions, it could guide the design of quantum computers and simulators that deliberately use instability to amplify signals or engineer how particles transfer energy—turning what is usually a problem into a useful feature.

Dynamic Optimistic Constrained OCO with Memory via Delay Equivalence

Making learning systems smarter when they remember and face shifting rules

Mohammed Abdullah, George Iosifidis, Salah Eddine Elayoubi et al.
arXiv:2611.08302

Summary

Researchers created an algorithm that helps learning systems make better decisions when they must remember past states and adapt to changing constraints—like a robot managing energy budgets while learning from experience. The system improves dramatically when it can use predictions, reaching near-perfect performance with accurate forecasts, and works without requiring unrealistic assumptions about how constraints behave.

Why it matters

Many real systems—from power grids balancing supply with shifting demand to autonomous vehicles managing fuel while adapting to traffic—must learn and adapt while respecting hard constraints. This work provides the first practical guarantees that such systems won't accumulate large budget violations over time, and shows how good predictions can nearly eliminate the performance cost of having to remember past decisions. This makes it possible to prove these systems will behave safely even in unpredictable environments.

StepBack: Step-Level Error-Localized Resampling for Efficient Test-Time Reasoning

Fixing AI reasoning errors one step at a time instead of starting over

Yang Ouyang, Jung-Eun Kim
arXiv:2611.08403

Summary

A new method called StepBack helps AI systems solve math and reasoning problems more efficiently by pinpointing where they first go wrong, then recomputing only from that point onward. It achieves the same accuracy as existing methods while cutting computational costs by up to 41%.

Why it matters

AI reasoning systems currently waste enormous computation by redoing entire reasoning chains from scratch. StepBack cuts that waste substantially, which directly lowers the energy and cost of running these systems at scale—a key bottleneck as reasoning-based AI becomes more common in research and applications.

Conformal Prediction for Time-Dependent PDEs

Making AI predictions reliable when simulating physical systems over time

Joshua Stiller, Annika Schneider, Eyke Hüllermeier
arXiv:2611.08409

Summary

A standard technique for making sure machine learning predictions come with honest confidence bounds breaks down when forecasting how physical systems evolve. The researchers proved why this happens mathematically and showed that fixing it requires carefully choosing how fine-grained your computational grid is, then proposed a weighted method that can provide genuine coverage guarantees even as predictions stretch across longer time periods.

Why it matters

When machine learning models replace expensive physics simulations in flood forecasting, engine design, or financial risk modeling, getting the confidence intervals wrong can mask dangerous blind spots. This work explains the failure mode and offers practical fixes, which means engineers and scientists can now build safety margins into AI-based predictions with better mathematical justification rather than guesswork.

Spectral Regimes of Truncated sinc-Power and Pulse-Train Kernels: Edge Exponents, Per-Edge Plunges, and the Landau-Widom Limit

How smooth versus jagged signal boundaries change mathematical behavior

Unnikrishna Pillai
arXiv:2611.08401

Summary

When scientists observe a limited slice of a signal, the math predicts a sharp transition zone between reliable and unreliable measurements — but smoothing the signal's edge fundamentally reshapes this zone. Instead of a narrow plunge, you get a gradual power-law decay, with the shape controlled by how much smoothing is applied.

Why it matters

Signal processing underpins everything from radar and sonar to medical imaging and wireless communication. Understanding exactly how the shape of a signal's boundaries affects which measurements stay reliable determines whether you can trust data from real-world systems that never have perfectly sharp signal edges. This work provides the precise mathematical rules engineers need to predict reliability for signals with realistic, smoothed boundaries.

A Framework for Context Enrichment and Selective Robustness in LLM Social Agents

Making AI social agents more reliable by grounding them in richer context

Mattia Greiche, Reihaneh Rabbany, Maximilian Puelma Touzel
arXiv:2611.06914

Summary

When researchers use large language models to simulate human behavior in social scenarios, small rewording changes can dramatically flip how the AI acts — a major problem for trusting the results. Adding specific types of background information to the AI's prompts — like clear rules, realistic scenarios, or explanations of what matters in a decision — reduced this flip-flopping and made behavior more predictable, though different types of context worked better for different AI models.

Why it matters

Social simulations using AI are increasingly used to forecast human behavior, test policy ideas, and design systems. If these simulations give wildly different results from trivial prompt changes, they're unreliable for real-world decisions. This framework provides a concrete way to test and improve whether AI agents are stable enough to trust, which is essential before using them to guide anything from public health to economics.
