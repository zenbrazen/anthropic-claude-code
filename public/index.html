Shape-Aware Three-Way Merge for RDF Knowledge Graphs: The schema defines the conflict: an implementation and evaluation

Using database rules to automatically resolve conflicts when merging knowledge graphs

Steve Brown
arXiv:2611.34870

Summary

Researchers built a system that merges conflicting versions of RDF knowledge graphs by using the database's own schema rules to decide which conflicts are actually real. The system correctly identified 33 genuine conflicts across test cases while asking humans far fewer questions than existing methods — and crucially, it never silently lost or invented data.

Why it matters

Knowledge graphs are merged constantly in multi-agent systems where different sources update the same information. Current merging methods either lose track of real conflicts or overwhelm users with questions (the existing baseline asked 845 questions versus 33 actual conflicts). This approach cuts unnecessary human decisions while preventing silent data loss, reducing both the burden on people maintaining these systems and the risk of corrupted or missing information.

Schrödinger's Legacy: The Wave Function and Quantum Reality

How Schrödinger's rejected idea about electrons still shapes quantum science today

Davide Romano, Massimiliano Badino
arXiv:2611.34895

Summary

Schrödinger's original interpretation of the wave function—treating it as the actual physical density of electrons rather than a tool for prediction—was abandoned by mainstream physics nearly a century ago. But his core idea survived and evolved into modern approaches that physicists still use to understand atoms, molecules, and the fundamental nature of reality itself.

Why it matters

Quantum mechanics remains philosophically contentious because physicists disagree on what the wave function actually represents. Schrödinger's realist approach—the idea that the math describes something objectively real, not just what we can predict—underpins practical quantum chemistry tools used to design drugs, materials, and semiconductors. Understanding this intellectual lineage clarifies why some of today's most promising interpretations of quantum mechanics trace directly back to ideas physicists thought they'd discarded.

Adaptive Control Barrier Functions for Nonlinear Singularly Perturbed Systems

Keeping fast and slow systems safe when they have unknown parameters

Prashanth Krishnamurthy, Farshad Khorrami
arXiv:2611.34866

Summary

Engineers have developed a new method to guarantee that complex systems with multiple speeds of operation stay within safe boundaries, even when their internal parameters are unknown or uncertain. The approach works by monitoring both the slow changes (like a vehicle's position) and fast changes (like motor response times) simultaneously, and uses real-time estimates of unknown parameters to adjust safety guardrails automatically.

Why it matters

Many real systems—from autonomous vehicles to aircraft and industrial robots—operate at different speeds and have uncertain properties that engineers can't measure precisely. This method guarantees these systems won't violate safety constraints like collision boundaries, even when the system behaves slightly differently than expected. Without it, controllers either keep systems artificially restricted to stay safe (reducing efficiency) or risk unsafe behavior when reality doesn't match the model.

μ-Bench: Benchmarking Multi-User Task Completion in Shared Interaction Voice Agents

Testing voice assistants that juggle multiple people's requests at once

Qihao Lin, Guanxu Chen, Shuhang Zheng et al.
arXiv:2611.34920

Summary

Researchers created a benchmark to test how well voice assistants handle requests from multiple people talking simultaneously—a challenge existing tests don't measure well. Across 800 test scenarios, the best commercial systems succeeded at completing all users' tasks together only 62–65% of the time, while open-source models fell below 20%, often failing to track who said what or keep conversations organized.

Why it matters

As voice assistants move into shared spaces like homes and offices, they need to juggle conflicting requests, respect privacy rules for different users, and prevent one person from triggering actions another person didn't authorize. This benchmark exposes where current systems break down—especially in understanding who's talking and following multi-turn conversations—so developers can fix these problems before deploying shared voice agents in real environments.

First Order Real Resonant Digital Filters and A Novel Orthogonal State Generator

Building resonant filters that work with real numbers, not imaginary math

Edward J. Daw
arXiv:2611.34677

Summary

Engineers typically use imaginary numbers to design resonant filters—the kind that pick out specific frequencies in electrical signals. This paper shows how to build a real, working resonant filter using only real numbers and simple first-order math, then proves it works by testing it on actual digital signal processing hardware. The filter, called FOREST, performs as theory predicts despite the mathematical sleight of hand required to make it real.

Why it matters

Resonant filters are everywhere: power grids use them to monitor electricity, motors need them for control, and many physics experiments rely on high-quality resonators. A simpler, real-coefficient design means engineers can implement these filters more directly in existing digital hardware, reducing computational overhead and making real-time signal processing faster and cheaper across power systems, industrial controls, and scientific instruments.

Accountable AI Delegation in Banking: Identity, Commitment Controls, Decision Evidence and Recovery

How banks can prove AI payment decisions are safe and reversible

Walter Kurz, Besnik Alidemi, Marco Schmidt
arXiv:2611.27505

Summary

Banks using AI to approve payments face a hidden risk: an AI can pass its safety checks but lose authority to actually process the payment before committing it. This paper describes a contract system that prevents that failure by checking authority, spending limits, and recovery options all at once—and recording everything atomically so nothing can slip through the cracks. Testing shows the system blocks 23 risky payment releases that current banking systems would allow.

Why it matters

As banks automate more payment decisions with AI, regulators demand proof that systems can't lose money or dodge accountability. A payment that gets approved but can't be reversed, or that exceeds a customer's limit without being caught, can expose a bank to fraud losses and regulatory penalties. This framework lets banks demonstrate that their AI systems maintain control over money every step of the way—and can always recover from mistakes.

Stranger Things: When Objects Appear Without Their Typical Neighbors

Why AI vision systems fail when objects appear in unusual contexts

Siddhartha Gairola, Jiahao Xie, Anna Kukleva et al.
arXiv:2611.34881

Summary

Modern AI vision systems recognize objects worse when they appear alongside unexpected neighbors—a problem called typicality bias that persists even in the latest, largest models. Researchers measured this bias across five types of vision AI and found performance gaps of 5–11 percentage points when objects appeared outside their typical company, then showed that a targeted data augmentation technique called Anchor-Atypicality Sampling could cut those gaps by up to 23 percentage points without hurting overall accuracy.

Why it matters

Vision systems deployed in real-world settings—autonomous vehicles, medical imaging, surveillance—encounter unusual object combinations that training data rarely prepared them for. A car detector that only works reliably when cars are near roads, or a person detector that fails when someone stands alone in a field, creates genuine safety and reliability risks. This work provides both a way to measure and diagnose the problem and a practical fix that doesn't require expensive retraining from scratch.

Make the Adversary Spend More: Stateful Deception as a Cost-Exchange Primitive for AI-Era Cyber Defense

Making hackers waste time and money through smarter AI-powered fake systems

Alessandro Cantelli-Forti, Isabella Marasco, Hosam Alamleh et al.
arXiv:2611.34770

Summary

Researchers designed a new approach to cyber defense that judges deception not by how convincing it is, but by how much time and resources it wastes for attackers. By giving each hacker their own consistent fake environment that remembers their previous actions, the system forces them to spend significantly more effort — cutting the defender's computing costs by about 63% while making attacks more expensive for adversaries.

Why it matters

As AI tools make hacking faster and cheaper, defenders are under pressure to match that speed with expensive computing power. This approach flips the economics by making attackers do the expensive work instead — forcing them to spend more time and resources probing fake systems that feel real to them. That shifts the advantage back to defenders, especially smaller organizations that can't outspend sophisticated attackers.

Sliding-Window Detection of Hidden Regimes under Stochastic Switching

Finding the right window size to spot hidden changes in noisy data

Arina Pambukyan, Hayk Nersisyan
arXiv:2611.34905

Summary

When you're trying to detect sudden shifts in data that's hidden by noise—like a stock market regime change or a system malfunction—you face a tradeoff: longer observation windows filter out noise but risk averaging over a regime switch. This paper shows exactly what that averaging costs, proving that when a switch happens inside your window, it causes an error with probability 1/2, and deriving the optimal window length to minimize total mistakes.

Why it matters

Real-time detection systems—from financial trading algorithms to industrial fault monitoring—must choose how much data to average over. Picking the wrong window means either missing real changes buried in noise or falsely alarming when you've accidentally averaged across a regime boundary. This paper gives practitioners an explicit formula for the optimal window size based only on how often switches actually occur and the noise level, eliminating guesswork from a decision that directly affects system accuracy.

Diverse Alternative Reasoning Traces for Hard Math Problems

Teaching AI to learn from its wrong math attempts, not just right ones

Lukas Aichberger, Adam Golinski, Arno Blaas et al.
arXiv:2611.34928

Summary

When large language models fail at hard math problems, researchers typically discard those failed attempts and try again from scratch. A new method called DART instead dissects failed solutions, identifies the weakest reasoning steps, and generates better alternatives for those specific steps. On difficult math problems, this approach recovers 37% more correct solutions than standard methods while costing roughly the same to run.

Why it matters

Training AI systems on math typically requires lots of correct solutions, which are expensive to find for genuinely hard problems. By mining failed attempts for salvageable ideas, DART cuts the cost of obtaining useful training data by 30% per successful solution. This could make it faster and cheaper to build better AI math tutors and problem-solving systems.

The Marauder's Map: Bézier Manifolds Reveal Hidden Surfaces for Model Merging and Ensembling

How AI models hide shared solutions in high-dimensional space

Abhiram Iyer, Mark T. Harnett, Sarthak Chandra
arXiv:2611.33353

Summary

Researchers discovered that multiple AI models trained on different tasks can be connected through a high-dimensional region of solutions that work well for all of them simultaneously—like a navigable map through competent middle ground. Using a technique called Nimbus, they showed this connecting surface is simpler than expected and allows AI systems to blend multiple specialized models while gracefully handling errors, outperforming traditional ensemble methods.

Why it matters

This approach lets engineers combine multiple AI models more effectively without retraining them from scratch, reducing computational costs and improving robustness when systems encounter unexpected inputs or corrupted data. The method mirrors how biological brains maintain flexibility and resilience—insights that could guide more efficient and reliable AI deployment in real-world applications where systems must handle varied tasks without breaking down.

The thermodynamic budget of objectivity

What it costs in energy to make events become permanently real

Wonyong Chung
arXiv:2611.34909

Summary

Facts aren't free — they require physical records, and creating those records always costs energy. This paper calculates exactly how much work is needed to make an event become objective fact (witnessed by enough independent observers that no one can erase it), showing the cost scales linearly with how many copies of the record exist and how complete the information is.

Why it matters

Understanding the thermodynamic price of objectivity connects fundamental physics to how reality becomes fixed and knowable. In quantum systems, where information can be manipulated, this reveals hard physical limits: past a certain angle of misalignment, no amount of energy can establish objectivity at all, and an adversary who controls most records can keep facts from becoming real regardless of your resources.
