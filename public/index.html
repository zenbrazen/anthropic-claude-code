Emergent Biological Capabilities in a Foundation Model for Molecular Interactions

AI model learns to predict how drugs bind to proteins without being taught

Adi Mashiach
arXiv:2611.14504

Summary

Researchers trained a massive AI model called Bonbon on 3.2 trillion molecular sequences and found it spontaneously developed three new abilities it was never explicitly trained for: predicting how drugs work, pinpointing where they bind on proteins, and determining the type of chemical bonds involved. When tested in the lab on nine diseases, the model identified entirely new drug candidates that actually worked, including compounds that inhibited proteins researchers hadn't specifically trained it on.

Why it matters

Drug discovery today is slow and expensive—identifying promising candidates can take years. Bonbon ran at five thousand times faster than current best methods while discovering genuinely novel compounds in real wet-lab experiments. If the 61.5% hit rate holds across more targets, this could dramatically accelerate the identification of new treatments for diseases where current options are limited or failing.

DeepFinance: Can AI Diligence a $100MM Deal?

Can AI handle the judgment calls that make or break hundred-million-dollar deals?

Drew Hahn, Tyler Shibata, Jackson Grove et al.
arXiv:2611.10926

Summary

Researchers created a realistic test of whether AI can do the complex thinking required in private equity and investment banking deals worth over $100 million. The best AI model passed only 57.6% of the criteria, and it frequently made the right calculations from the wrong data or found important information without acting on it—suggesting that AI still struggles with the judgment calls that matter most in high-stakes finance.

Why it matters

Banks and investment firms are already using AI to speed up due diligence on major deals, but this research shows these systems can confidently produce wrong answers by pulling from bad sources or ignoring critical evidence. Until AI closes this gap, dealmakers need to treat AI outputs as a starting point requiring human oversight, not as a shortcut to faster decision-making on deals where errors can cost millions.

One Curve, Different Weights: AUC, the Brier Score, Decision-Weighted AUC, and the Policy Youden Index

Why different ways of measuring model performance rank them differently

Yegor Baranovski
arXiv:2611.14465

Summary

Two standard measures for evaluating risk prediction models—AUC and the Brier score—often disagree about which model performs better. Researchers discovered that both metrics, along with a new weighted variant, are actually different ways of averaging the same underlying curve, but they put emphasis on different parts of it depending on what matters to the person using the model.

Why it matters

When hospitals, lenders, or other organizations choose prediction models to guide high-stakes decisions, they need metrics that reflect what actually matters to them—not generic measures that treat all situations equally. This work shows that existing metrics can hide these differences in priorities, potentially leading organizations to pick models that optimize for the wrong things. The new weighted metric lets decision-makers explicitly state what they care about and choose models accordingly.

Pyrite: Building and Benchmarking Protein-Ligand Pose Ranking Agents

Training AI to pick the right molecular shapes for drug design

Megha Srivastava, Michael H. Lim, Allison M. Keys et al.
arXiv:2611.14511

Summary

Predicting which way a drug molecule will bind to its target protein is surprisingly hard—even expert chemists with visualization tools perform barely better than guessing. Researchers created Pyrite, a benchmark of 1,500 test cases, and built two AI agents that solve this problem by reasoning about molecular geometry and learning which chemical features matter most, achieving up to 74% accuracy and outperforming human experts.

Why it matters

Drug discovery currently wastes time and money evaluating binding poses that won't work. An AI system that reliably ranks these candidates could accelerate early-stage drug screening and reduce failed experiments, potentially shortening the timeline from molecular design to testable compounds.

The Dose Makes the Poison: Exposure-Aware Evaluation of Generative Drug Design

Why promising drug candidates fail when you ignore how much patients must take

Maria Franz, N. Benjamin Erichson
arXiv:2611.13398

Summary

Computational models that design new drug molecules often overlook a critical real-world factor: the dose patients would actually need to take. Researchers re-evaluated 60 drug candidates using realistic pharmacokinetic simulations and found that 7 couldn't deliver enough drug to the target, while 28% posed safety risks at their required dose. Most strikingly, 85% of the candidates deemed viable turned out to be worse than molecules the initial screening had dismissed.

Why it matters

Drug companies rely on AI to narrow down millions of generated molecules into a manageable list for expensive lab testing. If the screening process ignores dose and exposure, researchers waste time and money pursuing dead ends—or worse, advance candidates that would require impractical pill sizes or dangerous safety margins. Incorporating dose-aware evaluation could redirect resources toward molecules that actually work in living bodies, not just on paper.

Prediction Inference for Hybrid Models on Realized Volatility Forecasting

Measuring uncertainty in stock volatility predictions, not just point forecasts

Kyle Parrella, Kejin Wu
arXiv:2611.13212

Summary

Researchers upgraded hybrid machine-learning models for predicting stock market volatility by adding a measure of uncertainty around each forecast—something prior work had ignored. The new approach combines ARMA statistical modeling with LSTM neural networks and uses bootstrap resampling to build prediction intervals that capture fat tails in financial data. Testing on U.S. stock indices from 2020 to 2025 shows the method produces intervals closer to their intended coverage levels than competing approaches.

Why it matters

Traders and risk managers rely on volatility forecasts to price options, set position limits, and hedge portfolios. A point estimate without a confidence band is incomplete—you need to know how wrong you might be. This work delivers prediction intervals with reliable coverage, so investors can size positions appropriately rather than making decisions based on false precision.

Contrastive Discovery: Open-Ended Scientific Discovery over Competing Explanations

How AI scientists can find the right answer, not just rule one out

Ziang Liu, James J. Kim, Yijia Dai et al.
arXiv:2611.14514

Summary

When AI systems test scientific ideas, they typically check whether a hypothesis is true or false—but rejection only rules out one option, leaving many alternatives possible. Researchers show that testing against multiple competing explanations instead resolves 73% of rejected hypotheses to a specific alternative, and guides AI to answer 67% more scientific questions with the same experimental budget.

Why it matters

As AI takes on more autonomous scientific work, moving from yes-or-no testing to hypothesis comparison could dramatically accelerate discovery by eliminating wasted experiments on dead ends. In validation tests with human experts, this approach achieved 78–82% agreement, suggesting AI systems using this method could propose ideas that real scientists would independently verify as correct.

Cotangent-lift inference of stochastic microbial dynamics

Untangling real bacterial growth from measurement errors in gut samples

José A. Capitán, Alberto Megías, Cristina Sardón
arXiv:2611.14423

Summary

When scientists measure bacteria in the gut over time, they face a puzzle: the noise from their measuring tools gets tangled with the actual bacterial dynamics. This paper shows how to separate the two by treating the true bacterial counts as hidden and using advanced statistical techniques, recovering growth rates and interaction strengths that simpler methods miss—though real microbiome data remains noisy enough that even this approach attributes most of a dominant bacterium's changes to measurement error rather than true biology.

Why it matters

Microbiome studies rely on time-series measurements to understand how bacterial communities actually behave and interact. Current methods used in the field can't distinguish measurement noise from real biological change, leading to wrong conclusions about which bacteria are responding to treatment or changing naturally. This work provides a practical way to do that separation, potentially improving how researchers interpret whether an observed shift in a patient's microbiome is genuine or just noise in the data.

Structure of correlations for the single-j model using quantum information theory

How broken symmetries hide and rearrange correlations in quantum systems

Javier Faba, Vicente Martín, Luis Robledo
arXiv:2611.14460

Summary

When quantum systems break symmetry to reach their lowest energy state, physicists thought this was how they maximize correlations while staying mathematically simple. By studying a realistic nuclear model, researchers found that different types of broken symmetry actually affect correlations in contradictory ways, and that restoring symmetry moves correlations between simple pairs to much more complex patterns.

Why it matters

Understanding how correlations hide in quantum systems is essential for accurately predicting nuclear behavior and designing quantum technologies. Current approximations in nuclear physics rely on symmetry breaking to simplify calculations, but if this mechanism doesn't reliably capture correlations as expected, physicists may need to rethink which approximations are trustworthy for real predictions.

Causal Concept Explanations for Deep Neural Models

Making AI explanations actually prove why decisions happen

Josh Rountree, Pulkit Verma, Oswin So et al.
arXiv:2611.14407

Summary

Most AI explanations only show what matters, not whether they truly caused a decision. Researchers built CCW-Net, a new method that forces neural networks to learn concept-based explanations that are provably necessary and sufficient for their outputs—meaning a concept only gets credit for influencing a result if removing it actually changes that result. The method measures exactly how much each concept causally contributes to each decision, eliminating explanations that look right but mislead.

Why it matters

When autonomous systems make critical decisions—like controlling aircraft or self-driving cars—we need to trust that their explanations reflect real causal chains, not coincidences. A model might output "plane safe to fly" while a dashboard shows "fuel level normal," but if that fuel reading doesn't actually drive the decision, the explanation creates dangerous false confidence. CCW-Net fills this gap by certifying that explanations are genuinely causal, making it possible for regulators and operators to confidently evaluate whether a system is truly reasoning safely or just appearing to.

Measuring Robustness and Efficiency in a Connectome-Constrained Fly Visual System Model: Linking an Adversarial and a Perceptual Illusion

Why fruit fly brains are efficient but fall for the same visual tricks we do

Alexandre Garcia-Duran, Alejandro Rodriguez-Garcia, Manuel Molano-Mazón et al.
arXiv:2611.14498

Summary

A neural network modeled on the fruit fly's visual circuitry processes motion detection using 100,000 times fewer computations than standard artificial systems, yet it fails in predictable ways—stumbling over the same optical illusions that fool biological vision. The network's vulnerabilities trace back to a single low-level motion computation that both adversarial attacks and the classic reverse-phi illusion exploit identically.

Why it matters

Brains solve the efficiency problem that plagues AI: they do complex visual work on a tiny energy budget. By identifying exactly where the fly brain's design trades robustness for efficiency, researchers can now test whether real flies share the same perceptual blind spots, turning artificial failures into predictions about biology. This also suggests that building AI systems around biological wiring constraints might help us understand *why* both natural and artificial vision systems fail in similar ways.

Cascading Contingency Analysis of Modern Power Systems Using a Stochastic Hybrid Systems Framework

Predicting how one power grid failure can trigger a dangerous chain reaction

Masoud H. Nazari, Hamid Varmazyari
arXiv:2611.14439

Summary

Engineers developed a new method to predict how a single power outage can cascade into multiple failures across the electrical grid. Unlike traditional tests that only check what happens when one or two lines go down, this framework reveals hidden domino-effect patterns that could leave millions without power—and it works by modeling how real stresses spread through the system after each failure.

Why it matters

Power grid operators use outdated methods that miss the biggest threats. This framework identifies which component failures are most dangerous before they happen, allowing utilities to reinforce vulnerable spots in the network. Better predictions mean fewer blackouts, faster restoration, and lower costs from unexpected outages.
