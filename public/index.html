Communication-Efficient Federated Learning of Latent Patient Representations from Multi-Institutional EHRs

Learning patient patterns from multiple hospitals without sharing sensitive records

Zhiyu Yan, Shiao Liu, Rahul Mahajan et al.
arXiv:2611.27900

Summary

Researchers developed a method that lets hospitals collaborate on identifying patient subgroups from electronic health records while keeping individual patient data private and minimizing data transfer between sites. Applied to stroke patients across multiple facilities, the approach recovered the same patient patterns that would emerge from pooling all records directly, while each hospital shared only aggregate statistics and mathematical summaries instead of raw patient information.

Why it matters

Hospitals often can't combine patient records due to privacy rules and technical barriers, which means insights are trapped in silos and patterns learned at one hospital may not apply elsewhere. This method lets institutions participate in collaborative research without breaking privacy agreements or overwhelming networks with massive file transfers—making it practical for hospitals to build better diagnostic tools and treatment strategies that work across diverse patient populations.

The Rise of Agents and the Human Labor Market

AI agents are already shrinking job openings, especially entry-level roles

Nicola Borri, Anton Cheremukhin, Yukun Liu et al.
arXiv:2611.27210

Summary

Firms most exposed to agentic AI—the kind that can autonomously complete tasks—posted 2 to 3 percent fewer job openings over the following six to nine months. The effect was sharper after Claude Code 2.0's release, when affected firms cut job postings by nearly 5 percent. Crucially, the decline hit non-senior positions hardest; senior roles showed no significant decline, suggesting AI agents are replacing routine work rather than eliminating jobs wholesale.

Why it matters

This is the first large-scale evidence that AI agents, not just AI tools, are already affecting real hiring decisions at American companies. If the trend continues and spreads, entry-level and junior positions—traditionally the pipeline for career advancement—could become scarcer, potentially widening opportunity gaps. The finding also suggests that mid-career and senior workers may be insulated, at least for now, reshaping which workers face the most immediate pressure to adapt.

On the Complete Feature Set for ADHD Classification in Children and Adolescents Based on EEG Signals

A standardized brain-wave dataset to test ADHD detection methods fairly

Javier Sanchis, Álvaro Navarro, Jorge García-Carrasco et al.
arXiv:2611.27970

Summary

Researchers created the first large, carefully curated dataset of brain recordings from 156 children and adolescents performing different tasks, with 115 different measurements of electrical brain activity already calculated and ready to use. This allows scientists to fairly compare different methods for detecting ADHD from brain waves using the same data, rather than each team processing recordings differently and making results impossible to compare.

Why it matters

ADHD diagnosis currently relies on behavioral observation and questionnaires, which are subjective and often inconsistent. This standardized dataset removes hidden choices in how brain recordings are prepared and analyzed, letting researchers build and test ADHD detection tools on level ground. It means future studies can focus on what actually works instead of spending months re-processing the same raw data different ways.

Democratizing Cross-Project Knowledge for Transforming Water Governance and Climate Resilience: an Agentic AI Ecosystem

AI that finds buried climate knowledge across European research projects

Oriol Alàs, Ian Palacín, Iván Cester et al.
arXiv:2611.26398

Summary

Researchers built an AI system that searches through thousands of scattered research documents from EU water and climate projects, finding relevant information 26.7% more reliably than conventional search methods. The system uses specialized AI models trained on climate and water data, plus a smart controller that decides where to look next based on what it has already found.

Why it matters

Water managers and climate planners often make decisions without knowing what other projects have already learned — not because the knowledge doesn't exist, but because it's buried in hundreds of unconnected reports. This system lets them ask questions and get targeted answers from across multiple projects at once, reducing duplicated work and helping cities and regions respond faster to water scarcity and climate threats.

Neckpinches in a Positively Curved Market: Ricci Flow with Surgery on the Nikkei-225

Finding hidden groups of stocks by measuring how curved their relationships are

Siddharta Laloux, Bhargavi Srinivasan
arXiv:2611.25860

Summary

Researchers used a mathematical technique called Ricci flow to map the hidden geometry of relationships between 226 stocks in Japan's Nikkei-225 index. The method automatically discovered 29 natural groups of stocks that almost perfectly align with industry sectors—banks clustered with banks, insurers with insurers, shipping companies together—without being told what industries existed.

Why it matters

Stock traders and portfolio managers typically group companies by hand-labeled industry categories, but this method finds real clusters from the actual data, which could reveal overlooked relationships between stocks. Because each cluster turned out to be internally more correlated than with the rest of the market, investors could use this to build more effective diversified portfolios or spot when stocks are behaving unexpectedly.

When Should Deployed BCI Decoders Adapt? A Resource-Priced Ladder for Frozen EEG Trunks

Should brain implants automatically retune themselves, or stay put?

Sergey Musienko
arXiv:2611.25315

Summary

Brain-computer interfaces that read brain signals often need to adjust as the user's brain patterns drift over time—but constantly retuning wastes resources and can backfire on clean data. This study shows that adaptation should be selective and capped: a lightweight decision layer picks when to retune and how much, cutting unnecessary adjustments by 87% on stable sessions while matching accuracy of full retraining at 23 times faster speed.

Why it matters

Brain implants currently either retune blindly (wasting power and time) or never adjust (losing accuracy). A smarter control system that decides *when* to adapt means implanted decoders can run longer on battery, respond faster to real drift, and avoid false corrections when nothing has changed—all without harming accuracy.

GLiDE: A Fast, Calibrated Decision Model Distilled from Reasoning Teachers

Teaching AI to make fast, confident choices without explaining its reasoning

Sahibzada Allahyar, Julia White, Dhruv Atreja et al.
arXiv:2611.26509

Summary

Researchers created GLiDE, a family of AI models that can score and pick between answers in a single pass—without generating text or step-by-step reasoning. By learning from reasoning models through a technique called distillation, GLiDE kept much of the accuracy boost that comes from reasoning while staying 10 to 100 times faster, with the largest version reaching 73.37% accuracy on a standard benchmark while maintaining reliable confidence calibration.

Why it matters

Real-time decision-making systems like search results, recommendations, or customer service chatbots currently face a hard trade-off: reason carefully and be slow, or answer fast and risk mistakes. GLiDE collapses that trade-off, making it possible to deploy high-accuracy, confidence-aware decisions in latency-critical settings like mobile apps or live services where waiting 5+ seconds for an answer isn't an option.

Vibe-Trading: Towards Agent-Native Trading

Building AI traders that remember why they made each decision

Haozhe Wu, Zhonghang Li, Chao Huang
arXiv:2611.24845

Summary

Financial markets move too fast for AI agents to work reliably if they lose track of which data fed which decision. Vibe-Trading is a system that keeps trading agents anchored to the specific time, information, and reasoning behind each recommendation—so when prices change, traders know exactly what's become stale and what still holds. It chains together market research, strategy testing, and execution while maintaining an audit trail that lets humans verify and approve trades before they happen.

Why it matters

AI-powered trading is already happening, but most systems treat each decision as disconnected from its context—risking costly mistakes when conditions shift. Vibe-Trading's traceability and verification layer gives traders and regulators a way to understand why an agent acted, replay its logic, and catch dangerous reasoning before money moves. This matters especially for high-frequency decisions where a stale signal or misaligned authority can wipe out capital in seconds.

Simple Private Noisy SGD is Minimax-Optimal under Population Strong Convexity

When privacy protections don't slow down machine learning as much as we thought

Valentin Roth, Marco Avella Medina
arXiv:2611.26515

Summary

Adding noise to protect privacy in machine learning doesn't have to make models less accurate than the best possible performance. Researchers proved that a simple noise-injection technique called Noisy SGD can achieve optimal accuracy rates even when privacy is required, matching what the best non-private algorithms achieve. For logistic regression—a workhorse method in statistics—this eliminates gaps in earlier analyses.

Why it matters

Machine learning systems increasingly handle sensitive data like medical records and financial information. If privacy safeguards forced companies to accept significantly lower accuracy, the tradeoff might be too steep to use them. This result shows the safeguards don't come with the efficiency penalty researchers feared, making it practical to train accurate models on private data without sacrificing statistical performance.

AgentRoboCritic: Understanding the Capability of Frontier Agents on Direct Robot Control

What makes advanced AI robots succeed or fail at manipulation tasks

Yiyang Du, Yuxuan Kuang, Qitao Zhao et al.
arXiv:2611.26613

Summary

Researchers used a new technique called AgentRoboCritic to analyze how frontier AI agents like GPT-6 Astra control robots at the action level, rather than just measuring whether entire tasks succeed or fail. The analysis revealed these agents can recover from mistakes, understand objects correctly across different scenes, and perform complex manipulation without task-specific training—but still struggle with specific physical challenges like grasping and precise alignment.

Why it matters

As AI systems take on more physical robot tasks, understanding exactly where and why they fail becomes critical for safe deployment. This framework and dataset enable researchers to spot systematic weaknesses—like consistent failures at grasping—rather than just seeing that "60% of tasks succeeded," making it possible to target improvements where they matter most.

Visual Attribution Limits and Graded Provenance Auditing in Commercial Video-Generation APIs

Can you trust which AI model actually made your video?

Haocheng Mei, Yuchuan Tian, Yingte Shu et al.
arXiv:2611.26508

Summary

Researchers tested whether you can verify which video-generation service actually created a file you received, rather than a cheaper substitute. They found that only cryptographic signatures from one provider (BytePlus) reliably proved what was promised, while visual fingerprints fail once videos are re-encoded or compressed—dropping from 84% accuracy to 45% after basic processing.

Why it matters

Video-generation APIs charge different prices for different models and quality tiers, but intermediaries could pocket the difference by substituting cheaper services without detection. This work shows what auditing can and cannot prove, helping customers and regulators distinguish real fraud from legitimate processing, and revealing that most providers lack the authentication needed to catch deliberate substitution.

Optimal Control in Pairs Trading with Negative-Power Utility

How to bet on market mispricings without losing everything

Aleksei Kozhevnikov
arXiv:2611.26568

Summary

Mathematicians solved the problem of how to best invest money in pairs trading—a strategy where you bet that two related stock prices will move back together. Using a new approach to a classic optimization equation, they found explicit formulas for how much to invest and when, and proved these strategies never let your account go negative. The results show that when interest rates are low enough, your wealth stays bounded and predictable; when rates are higher, wealth can spike unpredictably, and there's a point in time beyond which expected returns become infinite even though the optimal strategy itself remains sensible.

Why it matters

Pairs trading is a real strategy used by hedge funds and algorithmic traders to profit from temporary price misalignments. This paper gives traders the first explicit, proven-optimal playbook for sizing positions and deciding when to buy or sell, without relying on approximations or simulations. The sharp prediction about when expected wealth explodes—even as the strategy stays rational—explains a real practical danger: at certain market conditions and time horizons, the math says your best move is still a risky one.
