Disentangling Spurious Correlations: How IRM Handles Diverse Shortcuts in Text Classification

Teaching AI text classifiers to ignore misleading patterns and learn what actually matters

Paul Grunenwald, Benoît Crabbé, Guillaume Wisniewski et al.
arXiv:2611.17107

Summary

Text classifiers often latch onto lazy shortcuts—like dialect or actor names—instead of learning genuine linguistic patterns, causing them to fail on new data. A technique called Invariant Risk Minimization (IRM) can suppress these spurious correlations by identifying patterns that stay stable across different datasets, and new experiments show it works across realistic scenarios like gender-profession bias and name-based shortcuts.

Why it matters

Text classifiers deployed in the real world encounter data different from their training set, and exploiting spurious patterns causes them to fail. If IRM can reliably steer models toward robust features instead of shortcuts, it could improve how well language systems generalize—from content moderation to bias detection—without requiring hand-labeled corrections for every new problem.

The Logic of Factor Portfolios: Boolean Rules, Interactions, and Trading Costs

Why complex trading rules cost more than they're worth

Ziyao Wang, Svetlozar T. Rachev, Frank J. Fabozzi
arXiv:2611.09834

Summary

A mathematical framework shows that stock-picking rules combining multiple signals trade much more frequently and eat up their profits in trading costs. When investors blend two or more signals together—say, buying stocks that are both cheap and have strong momentum—they trigger trades roughly 4 to 16 times more often than single-signal rules, and the added profits almost never justify the expense. Over the past 30 years, 58% of simple single-signal strategies beat their trading costs, but only 6% of strategies combining three signals do.

Why it matters

This finding challenges a widespread belief among fund managers that combining multiple signals into a single decision rule improves returns. The research shows that blending signals creates hidden drag: the more sophisticated the rule, the more often it trades, and each trade silently erodes gains. For practical investors, this means a simple rule—buy the cheapest stocks, or the strongest momentum—often outperforms elaborate multi-factor combinations after costs are paid, even though those combinations look smarter on paper.

Metal Sandbox: Isolated Execution and Command Scheduling for GPU Coding Agents on Apple Silicon

Running AI coding assistants faster on Mac computers by sharing GPU time smartly

Haozhe Wang, Lina Chen, Fanqi Wan et al.
arXiv:2611.17294

Summary

Researchers built Metal Sandbox, a system that lets multiple AI agents write GPU code on Mac computers simultaneously by sharing processing power and moving tasks between machines when GPUs sit idle. Testing on four Mac mini computers showed the system completed tasks 1.7 to 2 times faster than before, and simulations suggest it could cut waiting time by nearly a fifth when running multiple agents at once.

Why it matters

Apple silicon Macs are increasingly used for AI development, but current setups waste GPU computing power because coding agents sit around waiting for results. Metal Sandbox lets developers train and test AI models that generate efficient GPU code much faster without buying additional hardware. Since the system is open-source, teams can immediately use it to reduce development time and cut costs.

Forbidden subset sums in a linear range: an inverse theorem and the Erdős-Graham irregularity problem

How many numbers can you pick before they're forced to sum to your target

Mateo Matijasevick
arXiv:2611.17205

Summary

Mathematicians solved a 50-year-old puzzle about which numbers can be left out of subset sums. When you pick numbers from a limited range, there's a predictable maximum size of set that *won't* sum to a target number — and this size follows a clean formula when the range is at least half the target, but jumps around unpredictably for smaller ranges.

Why it matters

This settles a famous open problem in combinatorics, but also reveals something deeper: subset sum behavior isn't random or chaotic—it's governed by hidden structures in how numbers divide. The inverse theorem at the core could help computer scientists understand when and why certain packing and selection problems become computationally hard.

Protein Language Models Fail To Capture Contact Predictions Under Invariant Relabeling

Why AI protein models aren't learning what scientists think they are

Paarth Batra, Aryan Keluskar
arXiv:2611.16881

Summary

Protein language models produce accurate predictions of which amino acids touch each other in 3D space, leading researchers to assume these AI systems have learned the same evolutionary patterns as classical statistical methods. But when scientists randomly relabeled the amino acid letters while keeping all the underlying information intact, the AI models' predictions fell apart—while classical methods remained unaffected. This reveals that the neural networks are relying on memorized amino acid identities rather than genuinely understanding the coevolutionary logic beneath them.

Why it matters

Protein structure prediction underpins drug discovery and synthetic biology. If these widely-used AI models are succeeding for the wrong reasons—pattern-matching familiar letters rather than learning true biological principles—they may fail on novel proteins or rare amino acid patterns in ways researchers won't anticipate. The diagnostic approach introduced here provides a way to catch these hidden blindspots in other neural models before deploying them in high-stakes biological applications.

Negative-Only Policy Optimization for One-Sided Verifiable Rewards

Teaching AI to learn from what it gets wrong, not what it fakes getting right

Jiacheng Xu, Shuo He, Fuxiang Zhang et al.
arXiv:2611.17287

Summary

Most AI systems learn by being rewarded for correct answers, but in the real world, we often can't verify correctness—we can only spot when something is obviously broken. Researchers developed a new training method called NOPO that flips this around: instead of rewarding unproven "correct" answers, the AI learns by specifically avoiding the mistakes it knows are wrong. On tasks involving code, math, and logic, this approach outperformed standard methods even when perfect verification was impossible.

Why it matters

AI systems used for coding, math, and scientific tasks currently struggle when there's no reliable way to check if answers are truly right. NOPO makes these systems more trustworthy by preventing them from gaming partial checks—a common failure mode where AI learns to fool weak verification rather than solve problems correctly. This could make AI code assistants and math solvers safer to deploy in real systems where complete verification isn't available.

Preserve the Vulnerability, Break the Alert: Testing the Robustness of CodeQL Taint Tracking with Natural Rewrites

When code changes hide bugs from security scanners

Zhezhen Cao, Weiyuan Ding, Bowen Xu
arXiv:2611.17123

Summary

Security tools like CodeQL can be tricked into missing vulnerabilities through simple, realistic code rewrites. Researchers used AI to rewrite functions in ways that preserve actual security flaws while making the tools stop reporting them—silencing 26% of real vulnerabilities in tested code with just three rewrites each. The same AI, asked only to refactor code without targeting the alerts, silenced none, showing these gaps aren't accidents but predictable weaknesses in how the tools model code.

Why it matters

Developers often assume a vulnerability is fixed when an alert disappears after code changes—but this research shows that assumption is dangerous. If a tool can be silently bypassed by ordinary refactoring, security teams lose trust in their gates, and real flaws slip into production. The study maps seven specific gaps in CodeQL's design and shows the same weaknesses affect other analyzers too, giving security tool builders concrete targets to fix.

AgentSSL: Can MLE Agents Leverage Unlabeled Data?

Can AI agents find better ways to learn from mostly unlabeled data?

Ethan Lin, Akanksha Sarkar, Ziang Liu et al.
arXiv:2611.17323

Summary

Researchers tested whether AI agents could automatically design machine learning systems that work well with very little labeled data—a common real-world problem. The agents discovered novel combinations of existing techniques that beat human-designed approaches in two-thirds of test cases, improving performance by up to 22%, but struggled significantly when deciding which solutions to keep.

Why it matters

Most machine learning advances assume plentiful labeled data, but companies and researchers often have vast amounts of unlabeled information they cannot use. If AI agents can reliably automate the discovery of semi-supervised learning strategies, teams could unlock value from data sitting unused today—saving both the months spent manually engineering solutions and the expense of hiring people to label data. The bottleneck the researchers identified (selecting which agent-designed approach actually works best) is now a clear target for improvement.

Public measurements to test imaging technology that sees inside the human body

arXiv:2611.17225

Summary

The dataset includes measurements from realistic phantom models simulating the chest and head, along with the computational tools needed to develop and test new image-reconstruction algorithms.

By releasing standardized test data and example code, researchers give algorithm developers a common benchmark to improve on—similar to how public datasets accelerated progress in medical AI. This removes barriers for teams worldwide to build better versions of the technology.

Ensemble phase transitions in measurement-unitary hybrid quantum circuits

How measuring quantum systems reveals hidden transitions in information chaos

Saptarshi Mandal, Sthitadhi Roy
arXiv:2611.17322

Summary

When physicists measure qubits in hybrid quantum circuits—systems that mix normal quantum evolution with measurement—the statistical properties of the resulting quantum states shift dramatically at a critical point, even though the states themselves appear unchanged by standard measures. The researchers found that two different ways of collecting quantum states both undergo sharp transitions at precisely the measurement-induced purification threshold, offering a concrete physical explanation for why quantum information suddenly becomes orderly or scrambled depending on how often measurements occur.

Why it matters

Understanding these measurement-driven phase transitions helps explain how quantum information becomes ordered or disordered in real quantum computers and simulators. Since most practical quantum devices involve frequent measurements, identifying the exact mechanisms and critical points where quantum behavior shifts could guide the design of better quantum error correction and help predict when a quantum system will maintain or lose information coherence.

Emergent Biological Capabilities in a Foundation Model for Molecular Interactions

AI model learns to predict how drugs bind to proteins without being taught

Adi Mashiach
arXiv:2611.14504

Summary

Researchers trained a massive AI model called Bonbon on 3.2 trillion molecular sequences and found it spontaneously developed three new abilities it was never explicitly trained for: predicting how drugs work, pinpointing where they bind on proteins, and determining the type of chemical bonds involved. When tested in the lab on nine diseases, the model identified entirely new drug candidates that actually worked, including compounds that inhibited proteins researchers hadn't specifically trained it on.

Why it matters

Drug discovery today is slow and expensive—identifying promising candidates can take years. Bonbon ran at five thousand times faster than current best methods while discovering genuinely novel compounds in real wet-lab experiments. If the 61.5% hit rate holds across more targets, this could dramatically accelerate the identification of new treatments for diseases where current options are limited or failing.

DeepFinance: Can AI Diligence a $100MM Deal?

Can AI handle the judgment calls that make or break hundred-million-dollar deals?

Drew Hahn, Tyler Shibata, Jackson Grove et al.
arXiv:2611.10926

Summary

Researchers created a realistic test of whether AI can do the complex thinking required in private equity and investment banking deals worth over $100 million. The best AI model passed only 57.6% of the criteria, and it frequently made the right calculations from the wrong data or found important information without acting on it—suggesting that AI still struggles with the judgment calls that matter most in high-stakes finance.

Why it matters

Banks and investment firms are already using AI to speed up due diligence on major deals, but this research shows these systems can confidently produce wrong answers by pulling from bad sources or ignoring critical evidence. Until AI closes this gap, dealmakers need to treat AI outputs as a starting point requiring human oversight, not as a shortcut to faster decision-making on deals where errors can cost millions.
