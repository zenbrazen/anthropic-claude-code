CTLink: A Text-Prompted Segmentation Model for Linking Radiology Report Findings to 3D Chest CT Scans

Teaching AI to highlight what radiologists describe in chest scan reports

Mohammed Baharoon, Sung Eun Kim, Mahmoud Hussain Alabbad et al.
arXiv:2611.31222

Summary

A new AI system called CTLink can pinpoint abnormalities in chest CT scans by reading the radiologist's written report—automatically drawing boxes around the exact locations of findings like nodules or fluid that doctors mention only in text. On a major benchmark, CTLink outperformed existing tools by a large margin, achieving 81.6% accuracy at locating reported findings and matching the consistency of two independent radiologists reviewing the same scans.

Why it matters

Radiologists currently must manually review both the written report and the scan to verify findings match up, which is time-consuming and error-prone. CTLink automates that linking process, letting radiologists instantly see visual confirmation of each finding they report—catching mismatches, reducing re-reading time, and making it harder for important abnormalities to slip through unnoticed. The system is accurate enough to match inter-radiologist agreement, suggesting it could be deployed in routine clinical workflows without sacrificing reliability.

Towards Edge Artificial Intelligence for Predicting Antimicrobial Resistance in Mycobacterium tuberculosis

Running TB drug-resistance tests on hospital computers in poor countries

Nishat Tasnim, Faisal Ahmed, Rokan Uddin Faruqui
arXiv:2611.29143

Summary

Researchers developed a lightweight machine learning system that predicts whether tuberculosis bacteria are resistant to antibiotics by analyzing their genetic code—and it runs on basic hospital computers rather than requiring expensive supercomputers. The system correctly identified drug resistance patterns about 82% of the time across 20 different antibiotics, making it practical for clinics in low-income countries where advanced computing infrastructure doesn't exist.

Why it matters

Doctors in hospitals across Africa and Asia could get same-day answers about which drugs will actually work against a patient's TB infection, instead of waiting days or weeks for results from distant labs—or guessing and prescribing ineffective treatments that worsen drug resistance. This speed matters enormously: resistant TB already kills hundreds of thousands yearly, and faster diagnosis directly shortens treatment delays and reduces the spread of untreatable strains.

A faster way to price options in markets with sudden jumps

Filling in the motion gap between two first-person video frames

Gaoge Han, Yongkang Cheng, Shaoli Huang et al.
arXiv:2611.29218

Summary

Researchers created an AI system that generates realistic human motion sequences from two first-person camera images taken seconds apart—the kind of footage from VR headsets or body cameras. The system outperforms simpler methods by learning endpoint poses and smooth motion together in a single unified model, rather than solving each problem separately.

Why it matters

VR/AR systems and robots need to understand and predict human movement from first-person camera feeds, but existing methods require clean 3D pose data that wearables can't provide. This approach works directly from raw video frames, making motion prediction practical for devices people actually wear, which could improve everything from gesture recognition in VR to robots that work safely alongside humans.

Ziv Meets Sumcheck: Zero-Knowledge Proofs of Floating-Point LLM Inference

Proving AI models work correctly without revealing their secrets

Hassan Khodaiemehr, Khadijeh Bagheri, Chen Feng
arXiv:2611.29188

Summary

Researchers created a system to mathematically prove that a language model like GPT-2 produced a specific output without exposing the model itself — and crucially, they did it using the actual floating-point arithmetic that runs on real computers, not simplified approximations. They proved GPT-2 small's entire forward pass in 19 minutes, with verification taking just 21 seconds, and the system rejected all 236 attempted attacks.

Why it matters

Organizations could verify that an AI system is working correctly and hasn't been tampered with, without having to trust the company running it or give away proprietary model details. This matters for high-stakes applications like medical diagnosis or financial decisions, where you need proof a model isn't lying or manipulating outputs — and it now works on the actual arithmetic these systems use in production, not just theoretical toy versions.

Optimal isotropic three-material conducting composites with an ideal conductor, in the plane and in space.Bounds and optimal structures

Finding the perfect blend of three materials to block heat as much as possible

Andrej Cherkaev, Leonid Gibiansky
arXiv:2611.29235

Summary

Researchers solved a century-old puzzle in materials science: what arrangement of three conducting materials—including one that conducts perfectly—minimizes heat flow through a composite? They proved that the optimal structure consists of nested spheres with wrapped layers, and derived mathematical formulas predicting exactly how well any such arrangement can perform based on material properties and how much of each material you use.

Why it matters

This framework helps engineers design better insulators and thermal barriers for everything from aerospace to electronics. The mathematical bounds reveal fundamental limits on what any three-material system can achieve, so manufacturers know whether they're building something close to ideal or whether a different material combination might work better. The constructive proof also provides a practical recipe for arranging materials to reach those theoretical limits.

Communication-Efficient Federated Learning of Latent Patient Representations from Multi-Institutional EHRs

Learning patient patterns from multiple hospitals without sharing sensitive records

Zhiyu Yan, Shiao Liu, Rahul Mahajan et al.
arXiv:2611.27900

Summary

Researchers developed a method that lets hospitals collaborate on identifying patient subgroups from electronic health records while keeping individual patient data private and minimizing data transfer between sites. Applied to stroke patients across multiple facilities, the approach recovered the same patient patterns that would emerge from pooling all records directly, while each hospital shared only aggregate statistics and mathematical summaries instead of raw patient information.

Why it matters

Hospitals often can't combine patient records due to privacy rules and technical barriers, which means insights are trapped in silos and patterns learned at one hospital may not apply elsewhere. This method lets institutions participate in collaborative research without breaking privacy agreements or overwhelming networks with massive file transfers—making it practical for hospitals to build better diagnostic tools and treatment strategies that work across diverse patient populations.

The Rise of Agents and the Human Labor Market

AI agents are already shrinking job openings, especially entry-level roles

Nicola Borri, Anton Cheremukhin, Yukun Liu et al.
arXiv:2611.27210

Summary

Firms most exposed to agentic AI—the kind that can autonomously complete tasks—posted 2 to 3 percent fewer job openings over the following six to nine months. The effect was sharper after Claude Code 2.0's release, when affected firms cut job postings by nearly 5 percent. Crucially, the decline hit non-senior positions hardest; senior roles showed no significant decline, suggesting AI agents are replacing routine work rather than eliminating jobs wholesale.

Why it matters

This is the first large-scale evidence that AI agents, not just AI tools, are already affecting real hiring decisions at American companies. If the trend continues and spreads, entry-level and junior positions—traditionally the pipeline for career advancement—could become scarcer, potentially widening opportunity gaps. The finding also suggests that mid-career and senior workers may be insulated, at least for now, reshaping which workers face the most immediate pressure to adapt.

On the Complete Feature Set for ADHD Classification in Children and Adolescents Based on EEG Signals

A standardized brain-wave dataset to test ADHD detection methods fairly

Javier Sanchis, Álvaro Navarro, Jorge García-Carrasco et al.
arXiv:2611.27970

Summary

Researchers created the first large, carefully curated dataset of brain recordings from 156 children and adolescents performing different tasks, with 115 different measurements of electrical brain activity already calculated and ready to use. This allows scientists to fairly compare different methods for detecting ADHD from brain waves using the same data, rather than each team processing recordings differently and making results impossible to compare.

Why it matters

ADHD diagnosis currently relies on behavioral observation and questionnaires, which are subjective and often inconsistent. This standardized dataset removes hidden choices in how brain recordings are prepared and analyzed, letting researchers build and test ADHD detection tools on level ground. It means future studies can focus on what actually works instead of spending months re-processing the same raw data different ways.

Neckpinches in a Positively Curved Market: Ricci Flow with Surgery on the Nikkei-225

Finding hidden groups of stocks by measuring how curved their relationships are

Siddharta Laloux, Bhargavi Srinivasan
arXiv:2611.25860

Summary

Researchers used a mathematical technique called Ricci flow to map the hidden geometry of relationships between 226 stocks in Japan's Nikkei-225 index. The method automatically discovered 29 natural groups of stocks that almost perfectly align with industry sectors—banks clustered with banks, insurers with insurers, shipping companies together—without being told what industries existed.

Why it matters

Stock traders and portfolio managers typically group companies by hand-labeled industry categories, but this method finds real clusters from the actual data, which could reveal overlooked relationships between stocks. Because each cluster turned out to be internally more correlated than with the rest of the market, investors could use this to build more effective diversified portfolios or spot when stocks are behaving unexpectedly.

When Should Deployed BCI Decoders Adapt? A Resource-Priced Ladder for Frozen EEG Trunks

Should brain implants automatically retune themselves, or stay put?

Sergey Musienko
arXiv:2611.25315

Summary

Brain-computer interfaces that read brain signals often need to adjust as the user's brain patterns drift over time—but constantly retuning wastes resources and can backfire on clean data. This study shows that adaptation should be selective and capped: a lightweight decision layer picks when to retune and how much, cutting unnecessary adjustments by 87% on stable sessions while matching accuracy of full retraining at 23 times faster speed.

Why it matters

Brain implants currently either retune blindly (wasting power and time) or never adjust (losing accuracy). A smarter control system that decides *when* to adapt means implanted decoders can run longer on battery, respond faster to real drift, and avoid false corrections when nothing has changed—all without harming accuracy.
