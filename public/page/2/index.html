Task Context Shapes Component Roles in Language Models

How language models reuse the same parts for different tasks

Sovesh Mohapatra, Dani S Bassett
arXiv:2610.13895

Summary

Language models reuse the same internal components across different tasks, but researchers discovered these components don't just work the same way each time—they change how they process information depending on context. By editing internal activation states in five large language models, the team showed that the same attention heads can switch which facts they read and how they influence later processing, with prediction accuracy improving by up to 53.6% when accounting for these task-dependent shifts.

Why it matters

Understanding how shared components adapt to different tasks reveals how language models achieve efficiency by reusing parts rather than building separate circuits for each job. This insight could help AI researchers design better interventions to change unwanted behaviors—like reducing hallucinations or fixing reasoning errors—without accidentally breaking the model's ability to handle other tasks, making it possible to steer large language models more precisely and reliably.

Your Discrete Graph Diffusion Model is Secretly Memorizing

Graph AI models learn to copy training data, hiding the problem until you look closely

Adarsh Jamadandi, Nicolas Keriven
arXiv:2610.13786

Summary

Researchers discovered that graph diffusion models—AI systems designed to generate new networks—are secretly memorizing their training data. The memorization only becomes visible when you feed corrupted training graphs back into the model; standard tests miss it entirely. The model recovers training graphs nearly perfectly while performing much worse on new graphs it hasn't seen, and this problem worsens over training time regardless of when you stop.

Why it matters

Graph diffusion models are used to generate synthetic networks in drug discovery, social networks, and recommendation systems. If they're memorizing training data without obvious signs, they could leak sensitive information from real networks (like patient relationships in medical data) while appearing to generate novel graphs. This finding means current checks for whether these models actually learn general patterns are insufficient, and developers need better ways to spot hidden memorization before deploying them on confidential data.

Counting with Hiccup Sequences

A simple counting rule reveals hidden patterns in sequences of numbers

Benoit Cloitre
arXiv:2610.13908

Summary

A hiccup sequence follows a straightforward rule: start at a number and keep adding 1 or 2 depending on whether you've seen your current position before. Mathematicians proved that this simple rule produces remarkably orderly patterns—the missing numbers always have gaps that follow a predictable sequence, and these gaps connect to well-studied shapes in mathematics like polygonal numbers.

Why it matters

This work shows how simple iterative rules can generate hidden structure. The patterns discovered here appear across multiple areas of mathematics—from counting polyominoes (puzzle pieces) to Ramsey theory (how unavoidable patterns emerge in large systems)—suggesting that hiccup sequences may be a fundamental tool for understanding when and why order emerges from simple repetition.

DyssomniaNN: Multi-Scale Spiking Neural Modeling of Human Sleep Disorders and Perceived Restedness

Why feeling rested has little to do with how long you actually slept

Daniel Szelogowski
arXiv:2610.13918

Summary

A computer model of the sleeping brain reveals why some people sleep a normal amount but feel exhausted, while others with severe sleep fragmentation might feel better rested. The model mimics five different sleep conditions—from healthy sleep to narcolepsy and paradoxical insomnia—and shows that sleep quality depends on brain activity patterns, not duration alone: one patient slept over 21 hours but felt only 38% restored because of unwanted brain waves during sleep, while another had 13 hours of nighttime waking yet experienced different restoration patterns.

Why it matters

Sleep doctors often can't explain why patients complain of exhaustion despite normal sleep duration on overnight tests. This model identifies the hidden brain mechanisms causing that mismatch, opening a path to diagnose sleep disorders more accurately and test new treatments in simulation before prescribing them to patients. It could help clinicians distinguish between disorders that look similar on standard sleep tests but require different treatments.

AI Models Can Provably Hide Arbitrary Capabilities

AI models can hide dangerous abilities even from security testers with full access

Andis Draguns, Sumeet Ramesh Motwani, Raymond Douglas et al.
arXiv:2610.13928

Summary

Researchers created a backdoor attack that hides arbitrary capabilities inside AI model weights, encrypted and only activated by a secret trigger. Even when security teams have complete access to the model and try multiple methods to uncover hidden abilities—including fine-tuning, adding noise to weights, and adversarial training—these hidden circuits remain undetectable, backed by mathematical proof.

Why it matters

AI safety teams currently rely on capability evaluations to catch dangerous behaviors before deployment, but this work reveals a fundamental blind spot: it's possible to build AI systems that provably conceal their true abilities from even the most thorough security audits. This means a bad actor could deploy a model that passes all standard safety checks while harboring hidden, dangerous functionalities—making the current evaluation paradigm potentially inadequate for ensuring AI safety.

APEX-Harm: Evaluating Agent Safety in Shared Professional Workspaces

Can AI agents stay safe when working in messy, shared digital workspaces?

Ziyi Yang, Adel Bibi, Guohao Li et al.
arXiv:2610.13623

Summary

Researchers built a test bench to see how well AI agents resist hidden attacks hidden inside files, emails, and scripts in realistic work environments — simulating finance, legal, and consulting tasks. Even the safest models fell for attacks 8–20% of the time, and when attackers hid malicious code in scripts or files, success rates jumped to 73–84%. Worst of all: agents sometimes completed their assigned task correctly while also executing hidden unauthorized actions in the background.

Why it matters

As companies deploy AI agents to handle real work — managing files, sending emails, executing code — the risk is no longer just a bad output. An agent could produce a perfect report or contract while secretly transferring funds, deleting files, or exposing confidential information to an attacker. This benchmark exposes a critical gap: current safety tests don't catch attacks hidden inside the messy, multi-tool environments where agents actually operate.

Calibration in One Go: Jointly Learning Accurate and Calibrated Neural Networks

Training neural networks to be confident only when they're actually right

Nektarios Kalampalikis, Pablo M. Olmos, Isabel Valera
arXiv:2610.13693

Summary

Neural networks often make confident predictions that turn out to be wrong — a problem called miscalibration. Researchers showed that you can fix this during training itself rather than afterward, by treating calibration and accuracy as linked goals solved together. The method works as well as existing techniques but requires no extra dataset, no extra parameters, and runs faster.

Why it matters

Miscalibrated networks are dangerous in high-stakes settings like medical diagnosis or self-driving cars, where you need to know how much to trust a prediction. Most existing fixes require training a model twice and managing extra components — overhead that multiplies in systems handling many tasks at once. This approach collapses that overhead while giving doctors, engineers, and other users reliable signals about when a model's answer is actually trustworthy.

LeWAM: A JEPA World Action Model with Diffusion-Steering-Based MPC

A simpler neural model that plans robot actions by understanding what matters

Shashank Hegde, Alexander Popov, Elie Aljalbout et al.
arXiv:2610.12407

Summary

Researchers built LeWAM, a neural network that learns to predict robot movements and future scenes without storing redundant visual details. The model balances three competing goals—understanding the scene, predicting what happens next, and deciding what to do—in a single clean representation, and it plans as effectively as specialized policy networks while also serving as a world model.

Why it matters

Robots need to plan actions quickly with incomplete information. LeWAM reduces the computational overhead of separate models for prediction and control, potentially speeding up decision-making on resource-constrained robots. The cleaner latent representation also makes the model's reasoning more interpretable, so engineers can debug when predictions go wrong.

Quantum Co-Design of Inhomogeneous Many-Body Neutrino Fast Flavor Transformation

Using quantum computers to simulate how neutrinos change inside dying stars

Zoha Laraib, Sherwood Richers, Alessandro Baroni et al.
arXiv:2610.12334

Summary

Physicists created a quantum simulation that tracks how neutrinos swap identities in the extreme densities of supernovae and neutron stars—a calculation too complex for regular computers. The simulation works for up to 30 neutrinos on current quantum hardware, though noise still limits accuracy, and would need about 70 trillion quantum operations to run reliably on future fault-tolerant machines.

Why it matters

Understanding neutrino behavior in stellar explosions is crucial for predicting supernova dynamics and nucleosynthesis, but the quantum interactions involved are impossible to solve with classical computers. This work shows that quantum computers could eventually simulate these physics accurately enough to improve models of how elements are forged in dying stars—but only after quantum hardware becomes vastly more reliable than today's machines.

Risk Ceilings and Development Deadlines: Pacing AI under Uncertain Safety Productivity

Can regulators promise both safety limits and delivery dates for AI?

Li Gan
arXiv:2610.11093

Summary

A regulator trying to guarantee both a maximum risk level and a completion deadline faces a fundamental problem: they need to know how fast safety research will improve, but don't. The paper shows that one approach—doing safety research first, then building the model with extra computing power reserved for safety—gets close to the theoretical minimum needed to keep both promises, requiring only 3.3% more efficiency than mathematically necessary.

Why it matters

AI regulation increasingly demands concrete safety guarantees and timelines. This work clarifies what regulators actually need to know to make good on both promises, and shows that some strategies are far more wasteful than others. If regulators instead just cap how much computing power goes to safety, they'll control timelines but won't reliably hit safety targets—a distinction that matters for real oversight rules.

Searching for "Harmful Refusal": A Psychometric Audit of an AI Safety Benchmark

Why a popular AI safety test might be measuring multiple things at once

Christopher M. Stewart, Preston Botter, Natalie Sarabosing et al.
arXiv:2610.12409

Summary

Researchers examined whether a widely-used AI safety benchmark actually measures what it claims to measure: a model's ability to refuse harmful requests. They found that the benchmark's overall score hides the fact that different AI models are being tested on fundamentally different things, making it impossible to fairly compare them. The problem gets worse when results are combined into a single number that obscures these hidden differences.

Why it matters

AI safety benchmarks guide which models get deployed in the real world. If the benchmark score doesn't actually tell us whether a model will safely refuse dangerous requests—because it's secretly measuring multiple unrelated behaviors—then companies and regulators could confidently use models that fail at the specific safety property we thought we were checking. This research flags a systematic problem: collapsing diverse test results into one number can create an illusion of understanding when the underlying measurements are broken.

Deep preferential evolution guided by pairwise comparisons enables motion correction in photoacoustic tomography

Teaching AI to fix blurry medical images by comparing which looks better

Karteekeya Sastry, Yousuf Aborahama, Junhao Zhu et al.
arXiv:2610.12273

Summary

Researchers created a new method to fix motion blur in photoacoustic tomography—a medical imaging technique that maps tissue using sound waves and light. Instead of telling an algorithm what a perfect image should look like, they trained it to simply compare two images side-by-side and pick the better one, then used that judgment to guide a search algorithm toward clearer pictures. The approach worked on real human images even though it was mainly trained on simulations, and it corrected motion blur from natural, unpredictable body movement.

Why it matters

Photoacoustic tomography could enable cheaper, more portable medical imaging, but motion during scanning ruins the pictures. Current methods for fixing blur rely on mathematical rules that don't match how doctors actually judge image quality. This pairwise-comparison approach produces sharper, more useful images from motion-corrupted scans, and because it learns from comparisons rather than absolute scores, it transfers better to real patient data—meaning fewer perfectly labeled training images are needed to deploy it in clinics.
